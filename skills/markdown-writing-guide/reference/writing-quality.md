# Writing Quality

Use these principles as passive reference when they fit the task. They guide prose choices; they do not replace the owning format, evidence contract, workflow, or review criteria.

## Fit Reader, Task, And Format

- Identify what the intended reader needs to understand, decide, or do. Make the purpose, answer, or action findable within the format's required structure.
- Include information when it answers the request, supports a material claim, enables an action, or provides context needed for comprehension or safe use. Remove details that serve none of those purposes.
- A planning pass can make that choice concrete: consider what the reader likely knows, what task brings them to the text, and what they should understand or do afterward. Set useful scope, name only exclusions a reader might reasonably expect, and outline against the intended outcome; remove sections that do not serve it. These prompts synthesize audience and scope guidance from [Google's technical-writing course](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/documents), [ATSDR's document guidance](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/pha-guidance/putting_it_all_together/tips-for-preparing-written-documents.html), and the [Department for Education's research-report guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67489b9d5ba46550018cebd8/Research_reports_guidance.pdf); they are adaptable, not required fields or a fixed outline. For optional task- or form-specific methods and examples, see [Writing Methods by Task](./writing-methods-by-task.md).
- Judge relevance, depth, organization, and voice against the task, reader, evidence, and artifact contract. Markdown may hold different genres and structured fields; its syntax alone does not determine the right content or voice.
- Keep specialized format requirements intact. Required headings, fields, order, schemas, terminology, evidence labels, and local style remain authoritative; apply general guidance only where compatible.
- For mixed work, apply these principles only to prose being authored or substantively revised. A path, class, or rendered Markdown output alone is not evidence that prose is being authored.

## Provide Supported Substance

- State material claims and requested actions clearly, then include enough supported detail for the reader to understand or act. Brevity is not a substitute for explanation, and a filled heading or field is not proof of completeness.
- Include steps, limits, qualifications, examples, or edge cases when they materially clarify use or prevent an unusable or misleading account. Omit them when they add no useful specificity.
- When a definition or bare fact leaves a relevant question unanswered, choose the explanation that fits: connect the new idea to something familiar, compare it with a relevant baseline, explain its practical meaning, or state a material condition, edge case, or unknown. These are options, not additions to make mechanically; use comparisons only when the basis is suitable. This menu synthesizes [Google's explanation guidance](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/documents) and the [Government Analysis Function's data guidance](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/writing-about-data/); its data-comparison examples are context-specific, not a requirement to compare every claim.
- When the reader must choose among alternatives, distinguish that decision from comparing an observed result with a baseline. As an editorial decision-writing method, identify criteria relevant to the reader's goal, compare options on a common basis only where the evidence permits, make material trade-offs visible, and state uncertainty or missing evidence that could change the choice. This applies reader-goal guidance from [Google's technical-writing course](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/documents) and evidence-to-conclusion practices from the [Department for Education](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67489b9d5ba46550018cebd8/Research_reports_guidance.pdf) and [GOV.UK evaluation guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/use-your-results-evaluating-digital-health-products); it is a synthesis, not a publisher-prescribed template. No matrix or single winner is required.
- For analytical claims, let readers inspect the relevant path from question or objective through evidence and interpretation to conclusion or recommendation. Distinguish observation or result from interpretation where conflating them could mislead. Make method, evidence quality, uncertainty, assumptions, or scope limits visible when they affect what follows from the evidence or how it can be used. This adaptable reasoning aid synthesizes the [Government Analysis Function's guidance](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/writing-about-data/), the [Department for Education's research-report guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67489b9d5ba46550018cebd8/Research_reports_guidance.pdf), and [GOV.UK evaluation guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/use-your-results-evaluating-digital-health-products); it is not a report schema.
- Tie material claims to supplied or inspected evidence; do not overstate certainty, scope, or causality.
- Preserve source-derived constraints and state evidence gaps rather than inventing a detail to complete the prose.

## Prefer Useful Specificity

- Prefer concrete actors, actions, objects, conditions, and outcomes when the material supports them. Explain technical or specialist terms when the reader may need that context.
- Choose detail by the question it answers for this reader: a supported condition, boundary, consequence, or next result may be more useful than another general description.
- Replace vague modifiers, buzzwords, abstractions, or metaphors when they add no identifiable meaning. Use technical language when it improves precision for this reader, not as display.
- Add an example only when it clarifies a use, boundary, condition, or expected result. Keep it relevant and evidence-faithful; examples should not be decorative or imply unsupported behavior.
- When available information does not support a useful specific, state the gap or keep the claim appropriately general.

## Condense Without Losing Meaning

- Remove filler, empty preambles, repeated rationale, and unrelated detail when doing so preserves meaning and use.
- As an edit test, ask what understanding, evidence, qualification, or action would be lost if a sentence or section disappeared. Cut or move it if it serves none of those needs; keep or tighten it if removing it would make the answer incomplete or misleading.
- Retain repetition when it prevents ambiguity. Retain context, qualifications, examples, and explanation needed for comprehension, evidence, completeness, or safe action.
- Do not target a universal word, sentence, or page length. A shorter text is not better if it omits necessary support; a longer text is not better merely because every section is filled.

## Fit Tone To Its Audience

- Choose directness, formality, terminology, and voice for the intended reader, task, and publication context. Avoid pompous or inflated phrasing and needless metaphors that add no useful meaning.
- Concision and precision should not make prose terse, impersonal, or compressed enough to obscure its meaning.
- Do not impose one house voice, formality level, grammar rule, or contraction policy across different audiences and artifacts.

## Use Structure Purposefully

- Preserve the owning format's required headings, fields, order, and evidence distinctions. Where the format allows choice, use headings that identify content and structure that improves navigation, comparison, or action.
- Avoid empty boilerplate and repeated information. Structure can make content easier to find, but it cannot supply missing substance.
- Keep the essential answer, reasoning, and qualifications on the reader's main path. Put optional depth elsewhere only when it serves a distinct need without obscuring what most readers need; signpost or link it so it remains findable. A separate section, appendix, or output is an option for genuinely different tasks or depths, not a default requirement. This placement advice synthesizes report-specific practices described by [ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/pha-guidance/putting_it_all_together/tips-for-preparing-written-documents.html) and the [Department for Education](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67489b9d5ba46550018cebd8/Research_reports_guidance.pdf); those sources do not establish a universal document structure or an effect on reader outcomes.
- Markdown or structural lint can establish syntax and format conformance only. It cannot establish relevance, evidence, clarity, or completeness.

## Review Questions

These questions can help assess a draft; they are not a required workflow, scoring system, or semantic test.

- Can the intended reader find the answer, decision, or action without disproportionate setup?
- Does each substantive unit serve the task, with enough supported information to understand or act?
- Are the basis, limits, uncertainty, and evidence gaps of material claims visible where relevant?
- Are details and examples supported and useful rather than decorative or invented?
- Can any repeated or inflated wording be removed without losing necessary context or completeness?
- Does the structure aid use while preserving the owning format?

## Basis And Limits

The external sources below are publisher guidance, not a controlled comparison or systematic review. They support descriptions of those publishers' stated practices; they do not establish a universal style formula or prove that one choice causes better outcomes in every context.

- [GOV.UK's user-needs guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/meet-user-needs/) ties content selection to a user need. Its [clear-language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/) and [tone guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/right-tone/) describe GOV.UK's public-service context, including specific wording and a voice that is brisk but not terse.
- [Google for Developers' global-audience guidance](https://developers.google.com/style/translation) addresses user needs, context, localization, and comprehension. Its conventions are written for Google's developer documentation and translation concerns.
- [Google's Markdown style guide](https://google.github.io/styleguide/docguide/style.html) discusses readable and portable source, maintainability, and simple syntax in Google's documentation corpus and tooling context.
- [MDN's writing guide](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/MDN/Writing_guidelines/Writing_style_guide), whose language guidance primarily covers English-language MDN documentation, recommends descriptive introductions and relevant examples. Its approximate 300-word target is qualified in its SEO discussion; it is not a general length standard, and MDN also warns against artificial inflation.
- [GitHub Docs' style guide](https://docs.github.com/en/contributing/style-guide-and-content-model/style-guide) frames choices around user value and context and explicitly applies to GitHub Docs.
- [Docker's documentation style guide](https://github.com/docker/docs/blob/main/STYLE.md) pairs concise writing with completeness in Docker's documentation context.

These sources express institutional editorial expectations. Their differences—including house voice, contractions, and length advice—should remain scoped to their audiences and publishing settings rather than combined into one universal rule.

## Illustration

The contrast is hypothetical and shows why a claim is more useful when it names an evidenced behavior. Do not reuse it as a fact unless the task supports it.

- **Vague:** “The update delivers a seamless, robust improvement.”
- **Specific, if supported:** “The update retries one failed upload, then reports the error.”
- **Hypothetical scope choice:** If readers must decide whether to investigate a reported change, retain the observation, a relevant comparison, and any evidence limit that affects the decision; distinguish the observation from a possible cause. Omit unrelated history. If no suitable comparison or causal evidence is available, say so rather than inventing one.
